**This is general information about official guidance, not tax advice.** Rules differ by country and change over time. Check with a qualified tax professional for your situation.

## United States (IRS)

The IRS treats digital assets as **property**, not currency. Practically:

- **Taxable:** selling for dollars, exchanging one digital asset for another, paying for goods or services with crypto, and receiving crypto as income (wages, mining, staking rewards, airdrops in many cases).
- **Generally not taxable:** buying with dollars and holding; transferring between wallets or accounts you own.
- **Gains and losses** are the difference between what you received and your **basis** (usually what you paid, including fees). Holding period decides short- versus long-term rates.

Every Form 1040 includes a **digital asset question** asking whether you received or disposed of digital assets during the year.

**Form 1099-DA.** Under Treasury regulations, US brokers report gross proceeds on Form 1099-DA for sales on or after 1 January 2025, and cost basis for covered assets acquired on or after 1 January 2026. Coins you move to self-custody and spend from there will not appear on a broker form, so your own records still matter.

## United Kingdom (HMRC)

HMRC’s Cryptoassets Manual treats most individuals’ crypto as subject to **Capital Gains Tax** when disposed of:

- **Disposals** include selling for pounds, swapping for another cryptoasset, using it to pay for goods or services, and giving it away (gifts to a spouse or civil partner are an exception).
- Gains are calculated using **pooling** (the "section 104" pool) with special same-day and 30-day matching rules.
- Crypto received as employment income or from mining/staking can be **Income Tax** instead.

GOV.UK’s guidance page explains when you need to report and pay.

## Network fees and taxes

Network fees are part of your cost. In general terms:

- A fee paid when **buying** can add to your basis; a fee paid when **selling** can reduce your proceeds.
- When you pay a Bitcoin or Ethereum network fee **in crypto**, the coins spent on the fee may themselves count as a disposal in some systems, even if they are tiny. The treatment depends on jurisdiction and circumstances.

The [daily log](/) records typical fees with dates, which can help you sanity-check records, but your wallet’s transaction history is the authoritative source for what you actually paid.

## Records worth keeping

| Record | Why |
|---|---|
| Date and time of each acquisition and disposal | Holding periods and matching rules |
| Amount and value in your currency at the time | Gains and income |
| Fees paid, and in what asset | Basis and proceeds adjustments |
| Wallet addresses and transaction IDs | Proving transfers between your own wallets |
| Exchange statements and 1099-DA or equivalents | Cross-checking |

## Other countries

Many tax authorities publish crypto guidance (for example Canada’s CRA, Australia’s ATO and Korea’s National Tax Service). Look for the official page from your own tax authority rather than relying on summaries, including this one.
